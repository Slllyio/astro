01 — Origin

How it started: the honesty problem

A model that is graded on the same examples it learned from will always look good. The only fair test is one it has never seen.

The engine had an early score of ~78% "accuracy." The trouble: it was measured on the very charts used to build it. That number was flattering itself.

So the whole project pivoted to one rule: test the engine only on worked example charts it was never tuned on, and compare its answer to what Raman himself printed. No self-grading. That single decision shaped everything that followed.

Two lucky facts about Raman's book made this possible at scale:

  • He prints two sign diagrams for each chart — the Rāśi (main chart) and the Navāṁśa (a finer division) — plus a birth line and a plain-English verdict. He never prints exact planetary degrees.
  • A planet's strength can be judged from those signs alone. Degrees and timing are a separate question. So we could rebuild a faithful chart from his two little diagrams — no telescope, no timezone math needed.

The key insight

Each planet sits in a printed Rāśi sign. From that, there's exactly one small "slice" of the sign that lands it in the Navāṁśa sign Raman also printed. We solve for that slice. The reconstructed chart then matches both of his diagrams exactly — Raman's own chart, rebuilt from ink.

02 — The core loop

The core logic, end to end

Every test follows the same shape. Read the chart two ways, and check whether the engine's grade lands near Raman's grade.

Three quiet safeguards do a lot of work here. A reachability gate throws out any chart whose two diagrams are geometrically impossible together (a mis-read). The word→grade map is pre-registered — locked before scoring, so the engine can't be graded against a moving target. And every corpus we grew went through a 3-agent adversarial audit, where independent checkers each try to disprove a candidate verdict before it's allowed in.

03 — The toolkit

What we built to make it testable

A handful of reusable parts, each designed so the measurement stays independent of the thing being measured.

Chart rebuilder reconstruct

Turns Raman's two sign diagrams back into a real chart, and drops any pair of diagrams that can't physically coexist.

Word → grade map pre-registered

Raman's phrases ("well fortified", "considerably afflicted") mapped to the 9-grade scale, committed before scoring. Fuzzy phrases are logged and skipped, never guessed.

Extractor agent

Pulls raw positions and Raman's exact words from noisy scans — never "features the engine would like." That's what keeps the two sides apart.

Five validators run()

One each for strength, timing, balance, longevity, and the calibration anchor. Every headline number is one of these, re-run live.

Blind growth pipeline 3 auditors

Extract → mechanical triage → three independent refuters → the frozen map → ship. It caught 5 bad "gold" rows that were in the data before us.

Drift guard tests

A test re-runs every validator and asserts the published numbers still hold — so the report and the engine can never quietly disagree.

04 — Inside the engine

How the engine actually scores strength

First the old way — add up points. Then the new way that broke a long-standing ceiling — apply Raman's overrides.

For each factor of a house — the house itself (bhāva), its ruling planet (lord), and its natural indicator (kāraka) — the engine collects findings: is the planet exalted or debilitated? in a good or bad house? aspected by benefics or malefics? hemmed in? and so on.

The original scorer simply added those up. It worked to a point — and then stalled at about 53% within-one for fourteen straight attempts to improve it. The problem wasn't the weights. It was the shape of the math.

The overrides, step by step

The new scorer walks a short ladder. It only ever lowers a grade — a strong start stays strong unless a hard condition fires.

The longevity result is the most quietly satisfying. The engine's reading of the 8th house (the house of lifespan) predicts Raman's ordering of who lived longest — while a generic "this is a strong chart" score actually points the wrong way. Getting the sign of that contrast right means the engine is reading the specific thing that matters, not a vague halo.

On strength, two changes finally moved a number that had been frozen for a year:

  • We plugged the doctrine in. Astonishingly, the 1,913 classical rules we'd carefully encoded were never actually used to grade strength — they fed only the written reading. Routing them into the score was the single biggest gain.
  • We changed the math from adding to gating (the ladder above), lifting unseen charts to 56.9% and the real-birth pool to 63.0%, and putting those grades on the live path.

06 — The wall

Where we lagged — and why it's a real wall

One kind of chart the engine keeps under-rating: strong houses that Raman credits despite a bad placement. We tried four different ways to fix it. All four failed — the same way.

Raman will call a planet strong because it's "highly fortified by the combined aspects" of good planets — even while it sits in a bad house. The engine sees the bad house and marks it down. That's the gap.

The natural fixes all share one fatal flaw: the very signals that mark Raman's strong-but-afflicted planets are equally present on the planets he calls weak. So any rule that lifts the first group also wrongly lifts the second. We proved this four times over, each result reproducible:

✗ Fortification floor
Reward heavy good-planet support. Every setting also over-credited the weak charts — net loss on unseen data.
✗ Dignity floor
"An exalted planet can't collapse." Fired on afflicted charts too — cost ~17 points.
✗ New structural features
Yogas, dispositor strength, benefic clusters — all appear more often on the weak charts. They point the wrong way.
✗ A trained ML model
Scored 0 of 8 on the hardest reference chart — even after we fed it strong examples. It learned the wrong base rate.

The honest conclusion

Raman's judgment here is holistic — he weighs this specific planet, in this specific house, against this specific company, all at once. That call simply isn't recoverable from any single feature of the chart, no matter how it's sliced. The wall is real, and now it's precisely mapped.

Two side questions closed the same way, cheaply: the engine's overall house verdict and its "from-the-Moon" reading can't be graded directly, because Raman states those in prose too soft for the 9-grade scale (they map on only 3 and 1 of 168 charts). And a transit (planetary-movement) track was scoped but set aside — his transit-at-event notes are too sparse in the available text to test against.

07 — The ML detour

The machine-learning detour

If the rule can't be hand-written, can a model learn it? We ran the experiment properly — and got a clear, useful "no, not this way."

Two experiments, both blinded and pre-registered:

  • An AI reading the charts cold. A frontier model graded anonymized charts (no names, no dates) against the fixed map, each paired with a scrambled "twin" to catch cheating from memory. Result: 47% — no better than the engine, and no memorization. The holistic read didn't beat the structural one.
  • A small transparent model over the engine's own findings. This one did score higher on familiar charts (63.5% vs 51.9%) — by discovering exactly the "piled-up affliction breaks it" gate we later hand-wrote. But on the strong-house reference chart it collapsed to zero: it had only ever trained on affliction-heavy examples, so it called everything afflicted.

The last lever, tested to the end

We gave that model everything it supposedly needed to generalize — the reference chart rebuilt in its own language, plus a fresh batch of strong training examples. It still scored 0 of 8 on the strong chart, in every configuration. Adding strong data didn't help, because the strong and weak charts look identical in its features. The model's one good idea was already in the live engine; the rest was memorized base rates. A pre-registered guard rail correctly refused to ship it.

This is a genuinely valuable outcome: the ceiling is learnable on familiar charts but not generalizable — the same wall, seen from the machine-learning side.

08 — Ahead

The road ahead

The cheap and medium levers are spent. What's left is a bigger idea — and it's clearly signposted.

The through-line of the whole effort: every route to beating the strength ceiling — hand-written rules, engineered features, and a fitted model — runs into the identical wall. That convergence is the finding.

The engine is faithful wherever Raman gave a crisp verdict, and the single place it plateaus is understood down to its mechanism — not hidden behind a friendly average.
